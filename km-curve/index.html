Before we build the curve

A Kaplan–Meier curve starts at 100% survival.

As we follow people over time, an event makes us recalculate survival. If someone is censored, they leave later calculations, but censoring itself does not make the curve drop.

The question at every event time is simply:

Of the people who were still at risk just before this time, what proportion survived past this event time?

Conditional survival

Suppose 8 people are at risk and 1 has the event. That means 7 of the 8 survive past this event time.

Conditional survival = (At risk − Events) ÷ At risk
(8 − 1) ÷ 8 = 7/8 = 0.875

This is called conditional survival because it asks about survival through this step among people who had made it to this time and were still at risk.

Before month 2, survival was 100%. So:

Survival at month 2 = Previous survival × Conditional survival
1.00 × 0.875 = 0.875 (87.5%)

Our KM curve therefore makes its first step down: 100% → 87.5%.

The big idea

At each event time, calculate the proportion who survived that step, then multiply it by the survival probability you had already accumulated.

S(t) = ∏ (1 − dᵢ/nᵢ)

nᵢ = number at risk immediately before the event time; dᵢ = number of events at that time.

Censoring does not cause a drop. It changes who remains in the risk set for later event times.

2. A smaller risk set makes each event matter more

The risk set is the group still under observation and event-free immediately before a time point. Move the slider to keep the same one event but change how many people were still at risk.

Your selected risk set40 patients at risk

Important nuance: Falling numbers at risk do not by themselves cause an immediate confidence-interval change. Greenwood's formula updates when events occur. At a later event, a smaller risk set can create a larger jump in variance. Reliability also depends on the number and timing of events and on whether censoring is non-informative.

Why events are counted first at a tie: A person censored at month 6 was known to be event-free up to that recorded time, so they belong to the month-6 risk set. Calculate survival from all people at risk at the start of that time, counting its events. Remove same-time censored people only when moving to subsequent times. This is the usual right-censored KM convention for tied recorded times.

What these confidence intervals do and do not mean

  • The shaded region is a pointwise 95% CI, estimated separately at each time using Greenwood's variance and the log-minus-log transformation, which keeps limits between 0% and 100%.
  • The interval is not a simultaneous confidence band giving 95% coverage for the full KM curve.
  • A long horizontal tail after heavy censoring is not proof of constant survival. Few remaining patients may provide little information.
  • KM estimation assumes non-informative censoring, meaning that, conditional on any factors used in the analysis, censored participants would not systematically have different event times from comparable uncensored participants. If dropout is related to prognosis, the curve and its intervals can be biased.
