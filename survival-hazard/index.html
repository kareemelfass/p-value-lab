Survival & hazard: five curves, one story

Survival, CDF, PDF, hazard, and cumulative hazard are different ways of describing when an event happens. Change the model or move through time and watch all five curves respond together.

Begin with one question

Imagine following people from treatment until an event. At month t, some have experienced the event and others have not.

Survival describes those remaining event-free. CDF describes those whose event has already happened.

Survived beyond t+Event by t= 100%

What do the other three add?

PDF shows where event probability is concentrated along time. Hazard measures the instantaneous event rate among those still event-free. Cumulative hazard adds that rate over time.

Important: a density is not a point probability, a hazard is a rate rather than a probability, and cumulative hazard can exceed 1.

Move the vertical line on every curve, or click a graph

1 · Survival function, S(t)

S(t) = P(T > t)

Probability of remaining event-free beyond t.

The curve starts at 100% and falls towards 0.

2 · Cumulative distribution, F(t)

F(t) = 1 − S(t)

Probability the event has occurred by t.

At any time, S(t) + F(t) = 1.

3 · Probability density, f(t)

f(t) = dF(t)/dt

How event probability is distributed across time.

Shaded area from 0 to t equals F(t), not f(t).

4 · Instantaneous hazard, h(t)

h(t) = f(t) / S(t)

Instantaneous event rate among people still event-free.

Its numerical value is a rate per month, not a probability.

5 · Cumulative hazard, H(t)

H(t) = ∫₀ᵗ h(u) du = −ln S(t)

Total integrated hazard up to t. Its shaded area is H(t), while the risk of having had an event by t is F(t) = 1 − exp[−H(t)].

Cumulative hazard is not cumulative event probability.

Lesson 1 of 5

One model, five equivalent descriptions

Watch the numbers update as you change time and model parameters. These are mathematical identities for a continuous time-to-event distribution.

Try three experiments

  1. Choose constant hazard. Notice that the hazard graph is flat, but survival still declines with time.
  2. Choose increasing hazard. Watch the hazard rise while survival falls. Notice that the PDF can rise and then fall, even when hazard keeps increasing.
  3. Set t = 8 months. Compare F(t), the shaded area under the PDF, with H(t), the shaded area under the hazard. They are generally not equal.

Model assumptions: continuously distributed event times, no competing risks, and no cure fraction. This lab plots smooth parametric Weibull curves. The non-parametric Kaplan–Meier estimator instead produces a stepwise survival curve from observed events and censoring.

Methodological references: Klein JP, Moeschberger ML. Survival Analysis: Techniques for Censored and Truncated Data. 2nd ed. Springer, 2003; Collett D. Modelling Survival Data in Medical Research. 3rd ed. CRC Press, 2015.
